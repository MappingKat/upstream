Origin Story

Presidents Day.
Peak demand.
A valve from 1974.

While thousands of skiers hit the slopes at Vail, the backbone of the resort was quietly failing. A critical air-actuated valve at the valley's wastewater facility gave out under peak load.

Our co-founder Rob — the District's Operations Manager — was the only person left who knew how to manually override it. He drove in on his day off, inserted a steel dowel pin, and narrowly avoided a permit violation.

That was during a normal year. In a water shortage year like 2026, every gallon matters more. Every treatment failure carries bigger consequences. And the operators who prevent those failures are more stretched than ever.

Standing there, Rob asked: "What other near misses are lurking in the shadows — and what happens when there's no one left who knows where to look?"

⚠ Water Shortage, Zero Margin

2026 drought conditions mean lower source water levels, higher treatment demands, and zero room for error. Utilities that waste water through operational inefficiency are the ones that end up on boil notices.

⚠ Knowledge Drain

When a veteran retires, their process knowledge — built over 30 years of droughts, floods, and equipment failures — walks out the door. There is no system to capture it. Tribal knowledge isn't scalable.

⚠ Data They Can't Trust

Operators are drowning in alarms from sensors they don't fully trust. They make decisions based on gut feel, not verified data — because nothing verifies it for them. In a shortage year, a bad reading can mean a bad decision with real consequences.

⚠ Enterprise Tools Don't Fit

Existing platforms (Xylem Vue, AVEVA) require IT teams and data scientists. A 3-person rural utility can't deploy or maintain them. The gap is enormous — and it's widening as conditions get harder.

"Operators don't trust their own SCADA data." — Josh Goldman, Metro Water Recovery
